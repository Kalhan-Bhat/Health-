## What it measures
Triglycerides are the most common type of fat in the body. When you eat more energy (calories) than you need straight away, your body turns the extra into triglycerides and stores them in fat cells for later use. Triglycerides travel in the blood inside lipoproteins, mainly VLDL. The test measures how much triglyceride is in your blood.

## Why the test is done
Triglycerides are part of the standard lipid profile. High levels are linked with a higher risk of heart disease, and very high levels are linked with inflammation of the pancreas (pancreatitis). Doctors also look at triglycerides in people with diabetes, excess weight or other metabolic conditions.

## Understanding the reference range
Many reports use bands such as: normal, less than 150 mg/dL; borderline high, 150 to 199; high, 200 to 499; and very high, 500 or more. In mmol/L, 150 mg/dL is about 1.7. Triglycerides rise for several hours after eating, so labs often ask for an 8 to 12 hour fast before this test. If you were not fasting, your result may look higher than usual.

## What high values can be associated with
Triglycerides above the range can be associated with eating shortly before the test, regularly eating more calories than the body uses, sugary foods and drinks, alcohol, excess weight, low physical activity, type 2 diabetes, an underactive thyroid, kidney conditions, genes and some medicines. Your doctor can tell which factors may apply to you.

## What low values can be associated with
Low triglycerides are not usually a concern. Very low values can sometimes be associated with an overactive thyroid, poor nutrition or problems absorbing food.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- Was I supposed to fast for this test, and could not fasting have affected my result?
- What might be raising my triglycerides?
- How do my triglycerides affect my overall heart health risk?
