## What it measures
Cholesterol is a waxy, fat-like substance. Your body needs some cholesterol to build cells and make certain hormones and vitamin D. The liver makes most of the cholesterol you need, and some comes from food. Total cholesterol is the sum of the cholesterol carried in all the different lipoprotein packages in your blood, mainly LDL, HDL and VLDL.

## Why the test is done
Total cholesterol is part of the lipid profile. It gives a broad overview, but on its own it does not tell the full story. Two people with the same total cholesterol can have very different amounts of "good" HDL and "bad" LDL. Doctors therefore look at total cholesterol together with the other parts of the lipid profile and with other risk factors, such as blood pressure, smoking and diabetes.

## Understanding the reference range
For adults, many labs print bands such as: desirable, less than 200 mg/dL; borderline high, 200 to 239 mg/dL; and high, 240 mg/dL or more. In mmol/L these are roughly less than 5.2, 5.2 to 6.2, and above 6.2. These are general guides, and your doctor may set a different target for you based on your overall health.

## What high values can be associated with
Total cholesterol above the desirable level can be associated with eating a lot of saturated fat, low physical activity, excess weight, genes and family history, ageing, an underactive thyroid, diabetes, kidney conditions, pregnancy, and some medicines. A high value is linked with a higher long-term risk of heart disease and stroke, especially when LDL is high or HDL is low.

## What low values can be associated with
Low total cholesterol is uncommon. It can sometimes be associated with an overactive thyroid, liver conditions, poor nutrition or problems absorbing food. Your doctor can tell whether it matters in your case.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- Is my total cholesterol a concern when you look at my HDL and LDL?
- What is my overall risk of heart disease?
- When should my cholesterol be checked again?
