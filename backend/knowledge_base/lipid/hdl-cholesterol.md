## What it measures
HDL stands for high-density lipoprotein. HDL particles pick up extra cholesterol from the blood and tissues and carry it back to the liver, which removes it from the body. Because of this clean-up role, HDL is often called **"good" cholesterol**. In general, a higher HDL is linked with a lower risk of heart disease.

## Why the test is done
HDL is part of the standard lipid profile. Doctors look at it together with LDL, total cholesterol and triglycerides to estimate heart and stroke risk. The ratio of total cholesterol to HDL is sometimes reported as well, because it gives another view of the balance between "bad" and "good" cholesterol.

## Understanding the reference range
For HDL, the report usually shows a lower limit rather than a full range, because higher is generally better. Common guides are: below 40 mg/dL for men or below 50 mg/dL for women is considered low, and 60 mg/dL or more is considered protective. In mmol/L, 40 mg/dL is about 1.0. Labs and guidelines differ slightly.

## What high values can be associated with
A higher HDL is usually considered a good sign for heart health. Very high HDL is uncommon, and in a few situations doctors may look at it more closely, but it is not normally a concern on its own.

## What low values can be associated with
An HDL below the recommended level can be associated with low physical activity, excess weight (especially around the waist), smoking, high triglycerides, type 2 diabetes, genes, and some medicines. A low HDL is linked with a higher risk of heart disease. Your doctor can explain what your result means alongside your other lipid values.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- My HDL is outside the recommended level. How does that affect my heart risk?
- How do my HDL and LDL compare with each other?
- What might be contributing to my HDL result?
