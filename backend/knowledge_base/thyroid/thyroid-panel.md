## What it measures
The thyroid is a small, butterfly-shaped gland in the front of your neck. It makes hormones that control how your body uses energy, which affects your heart rate, weight, temperature, mood and much more. A thyroid panel usually includes:
- **TSH (thyroid stimulating hormone):** made by the pituitary gland in the brain. It tells the thyroid how much hormone to make.
- **T4 (thyroxine)**, often reported as **free T4**: the main hormone the thyroid makes.
- **T3 (triiodothyronine)**, sometimes reported as **free T3**: the more active form of thyroid hormone.

## Why the test is done
Doctors order thyroid tests to check for an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) or an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism). Symptoms that can prompt testing include tiredness, weight changes, feeling too cold or too hot, a fast heartbeat, hair or skin changes, or a swelling in the neck. The tests are also used to monitor people on thyroid treatment, and during pregnancy.

## Understanding the reference range
A common adult TSH range is about 0.4 to 4.0 µIU/mL, and a common free T4 range is about 0.8 to 1.8 ng/dL, but labs differ. TSH works like a thermostat, in the opposite direction from thyroid hormones. When the thyroid makes too little hormone, the brain raises TSH to push it harder. When the thyroid makes too much, TSH falls. Ranges can be different in pregnancy and in older adults. Illness, some medicines and biotin supplements can affect results.

## What high values can be associated with
A high TSH, especially with a low free T4, can be associated with an underactive thyroid. High free T4 or T3, often with a low TSH, can be associated with an overactive thyroid. Mildly raised TSH with normal T4 is common and is sometimes simply monitored. Only your doctor can tell what your results mean for you.

## What low values can be associated with
A low TSH can be associated with an overactive thyroid, or with taking more thyroid medicine than the body needs. A low free T4 can be associated with an underactive thyroid. Your doctor will read TSH and T4 together.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- What do my TSH and T4 results say about how my thyroid is working?
- Could any medicine, supplement or recent illness have affected these results?
- Should I have the test repeated, and when?
