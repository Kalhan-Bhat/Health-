## What a reference range is
A reference range is the set of values a lab expects to see in most healthy people. It is sometimes called a "normal range". Labs usually create it by testing a large group of healthy people and taking the middle 95 percent of their results. This means about 1 in 20 healthy people will have a result just outside the range, even when nothing is wrong.

## Why ranges differ between labs
Each lab uses its own machines, chemicals and methods. Because of this, two labs can give slightly different numbers for the same sample. That is why every report prints its own range next to each result. It is best to compare your result with the range printed on the same report rather than with a number found online.

## Why age and sex matter
Some ranges change with age, sex or pregnancy. For example, haemoglobin ranges are usually a little higher for adult men than for adult women. Children often have different ranges from adults. A lab may print a range that matches your age and sex, or it may print several ranges.

## One-sided ranges
Some tests show only one limit, written as "less than 200" or "more than 40". For these tests, health guidelines set a target rather than a range drawn from healthy people. Cholesterol tests are a common example. Some reports also list bands such as "desirable", "borderline" and "high".

## What it means to be outside the range
A result outside the range is a signal to look more closely. It is not a diagnosis. Small differences are often not important, especially if you feel well and earlier results were similar. A result inside the range does not always rule out a problem either. Your doctor reads the result alongside your health history and other tests.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- Is my result only slightly outside the range, or far outside it?
- Does my age, sex or any medicine change what range applies to me?
- Should I repeat the test to confirm the result?
