## What a lab report is
A lab report is the document a laboratory sends after testing a sample, such as blood or urine. It lists each test that was done, the result, and a range the lab uses for comparison. Most reports follow a similar layout, even though the design changes from one lab to another.

## The main columns
Most reports are arranged as a table. The **test name** column says what was measured, for example "Haemoglobin" or "LDL Cholesterol". The **result** column shows your value. The **unit** column tells you how the value was measured, such as g/dL or mg/dL. The **reference range** column shows the values the lab usually sees in healthy people. A **flag** column may show letters like "H" for high or "L" for low when a result is outside the range.

## Sections and headings
Tests are often grouped under headings such as "Red Blood Cells", "Lipid Profile" or "Kidney Function". These groups help you see which results belong together. Some tests in one group are related. For example, haemoglobin, hematocrit and red blood cell count all describe red blood cells.

## Flags and comments
A flag only means the number falls outside the lab's range. It does not mean something is wrong. Many people have one or two flagged results that turn out to be unimportant. Some labs also print comments or mark very unusual results as "critical". A critical mark means the lab thinks your doctor should see the result promptly.

## Things to keep in mind
One result is a snapshot of a single moment. Food, exercise, sleep, illness, medicines and even the time of day can change some values. Doctors look at results together with your symptoms, history and earlier tests. That is why the same number can mean different things for different people.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- Which of my results are most important to look at?
- Are any flagged results something to worry about, or are they expected for me?
- Should any tests be repeated, and when?
- How do these results compare with my earlier tests?
