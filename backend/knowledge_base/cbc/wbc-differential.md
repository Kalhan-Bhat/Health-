## What it measures
There are five main types of white blood cells, and each has a different job. The differential (also called DLC, differential leucocyte count) shows what share of your white cells belongs to each type. It is usually given as percentages, and sometimes also as absolute counts.
- **Neutrophils** are the most common type. They are often the first responders to bacterial infections.
- **Lymphocytes** include cells that make antibodies and cells that fight viruses.
- **Monocytes** help clear away germs and damaged cells.
- **Eosinophils** are involved in allergies and in fighting some parasites.
- **Basophils** are the least common type and play a part in allergic reactions.

## Why the test is done
The differential often comes with a complete blood count. If the total white cell count is high or low, the differential helps show which type of cell is responsible. This points doctors towards possible reasons, such as a bacterial or viral infection, an allergy or another condition.

## Understanding the reference range
Typical adult percentages are roughly: neutrophils 40–75%, lymphocytes 20–45%, monocytes 2–10%, eosinophils 1–6%, and basophils 0–2%. Labs print their own ranges. Because the values are percentages, when one type goes up, the others go down. Absolute counts, if shown, are often more useful than percentages.

## What high values can be associated with
Higher neutrophils can be associated with bacterial infections, stress or steroid medicines. Higher lymphocytes can be associated with viral infections. Higher eosinophils can be associated with allergies, asthma or parasites. Higher monocytes can be seen during recovery from infections or in long-term inflammation. Your doctor can tell what applies to you.

## What low values can be associated with
Lower neutrophils can be associated with some viral infections, some medicines or bone marrow conditions. Lower lymphocytes can be associated with illness, stress, steroid medicines or immune conditions. Only your doctor can interpret the pattern for you.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- Which white cell type is outside the range, and what might that mean for me?
- Could a recent cold, infection or allergy explain this?
- Do I need the test repeated after I recover?
