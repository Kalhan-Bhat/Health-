## What it measures
A complete blood count, or CBC, is one of the most common blood tests. It looks at the three main kinds of cells in your blood. **Red blood cells** carry oxygen around the body. **White blood cells** help fight infections. **Platelets** help blood clot when you are cut. The test counts these cells and measures some of their features, such as the size of red cells and how much haemoglobin they contain.

## Why the test is done
Doctors order a CBC as part of a routine check-up, to look for the cause of symptoms such as tiredness, weakness, fever, bruising or infections, and to follow up a known condition or the effects of a treatment. It is a broad test, so it gives a general picture rather than a single answer.

## What the report usually includes
- **Red cell tests:** haemoglobin, hematocrit (PCV), red blood cell count, and the red cell indices MCV, MCH, MCHC and RDW.
- **White cell tests:** the total white blood cell count and often a differential, which shows the percentage of each type of white cell.
- **Platelet tests:** the platelet count, and sometimes the average platelet size.

## Understanding the reference range
Each part of the CBC has its own range printed on the report. Ranges can differ between labs and with age and sex. It is common for one or two values to sit just outside the range without being important. Doctors look at the pattern across the whole test. For example, haemoglobin, hematocrit and red cell count often move together.

## What high values can be associated with
Higher-than-range values have many possible causes, such as dehydration, infection or inflammation, smoking, living at high altitude, or other conditions. The meaning depends on which value is high and by how much.

## What low values can be associated with
Lower-than-range values can be linked with things like low iron or vitamins, blood loss, recent illness, some medicines, or other conditions. Only your doctor can tell what a particular pattern means for you.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- Which parts of my blood count are outside the range, and does that matter?
- Do any results need follow-up tests?
- How do these results compare with my last blood count?
