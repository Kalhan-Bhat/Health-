## What it measures
Hematocrit (also spelled haematocrit) shows how much of your blood is made up of red blood cells. It is written as a percentage. For example, a hematocrit of 40% means that red cells take up 40% of the blood's volume, and the rest is mostly the liquid part of blood, called plasma. Many labs in India call this test **PCV**, which stands for packed cell volume.

## Why the test is done
Hematocrit is part of a complete blood count. Doctors use it together with haemoglobin and the red cell count to check for anaemia (too few red cells) or for too many red cells. It may be checked when someone feels tired or weak, before surgery, or to follow up a known condition.

## Understanding the reference range
Ranges vary between labs. Adult ranges are often around 40% to 50% for men and 36% to 46% for women. Hematocrit usually moves in the same direction as haemoglobin, so the two results are read together. How much water is in your blood also affects hematocrit, so being dehydrated can raise it and receiving a lot of fluids can lower it.

## What high values can be associated with
A hematocrit above the range can be associated with dehydration, smoking, living at high altitude, some heart or lung conditions, and conditions in which the body makes too many red cells. Your doctor can tell which, if any, applies.

## What low values can be associated with
A hematocrit below the range can be associated with anaemia from many causes, such as low iron or vitamins, blood loss, pregnancy, long-term illness or kidney disease. It may also be lower if a lot of fluid has been given through a drip. Only your doctor can interpret the result for you.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- Is my hematocrit result in line with my haemoglobin result?
- Could being dehydrated or drinking lots of fluid have affected it?
- Do I need further tests to find out why it is outside the range?
