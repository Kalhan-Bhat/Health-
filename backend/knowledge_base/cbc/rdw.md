## What it measures
The red cell distribution width, or RDW, shows how much your red blood cells vary in size. In a healthy person, most red cells are about the same size. If some cells are much bigger or smaller than others, the RDW goes up. It is usually reported as a percentage (RDW-CV), and some labs also report it in femtolitres (RDW-SD).

## Why the test is done
The RDW comes with a complete blood count. It is most useful together with the MCV (average red cell size). Looking at both helps doctors narrow down the possible reasons for anaemia. For example, small cells with a high RDW point in a different direction from small cells with a normal RDW.

## Understanding the reference range
A common adult range for RDW-CV is about 11.5% to 14.5%, but labs differ. A slightly raised RDW on its own, with a normal haemoglobin and MCV, is often not important. The value means most when read alongside the rest of the blood count and earlier results.

## What high values can be associated with
A high RDW means there is a wider mix of red cell sizes. This can be associated with low iron, low vitamin B12 or folate, a recent blood loss or blood transfusion, the body making new red cells quickly, and some other conditions. It can also be seen when more than one cause is present together. Your doctor can tell what it suggests in your case.

## What low values can be associated with
A low RDW is uncommon and is not usually considered a concern. It simply means the red cells are very similar in size.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- What does my RDW suggest when you look at it with my MCV?
- Does a raised RDW mean I need more tests, such as iron or vitamin tests?
- Has my RDW changed compared with earlier blood counts?
