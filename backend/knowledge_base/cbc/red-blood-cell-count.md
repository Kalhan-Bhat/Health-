## What it measures
The red blood cell (RBC) count tells you how many red blood cells are in a small amount of blood. Red blood cells are made in the bone marrow and live for about four months. Their job is to carry oxygen from the lungs to the rest of the body using the haemoglobin inside them. The count is usually reported in millions of cells per microlitre (million/µL).

## Why the test is done
The RBC count is part of a complete blood count. Doctors use it along with haemoglobin and hematocrit to check for anaemia or for too many red cells. It may be done as a routine check or when someone has symptoms such as tiredness, weakness, shortness of breath or pale skin.

## Understanding the reference range
Ranges differ between labs. Adult ranges are often around 4.7 to 6.1 million/µL for men and 4.2 to 5.4 million/µL for women. The RBC count is usually read together with the red cell indices (MCV, MCH and MCHC). Two people can have similar haemoglobin but different red cell counts if their cells are different sizes.

## What high values can be associated with
A red cell count above the range can be associated with dehydration, smoking, living at high altitude, some heart, lung or kidney conditions, and conditions in which the bone marrow makes too many red cells. Your doctor can tell what applies to you.

## What low values can be associated with
A red cell count below the range can be associated with anaemia, which has many possible causes. These include low iron, vitamin B12 or folate, blood loss, long-term illness, kidney disease, problems with the bone marrow, and inherited conditions. Pregnancy can also lower the count. Your doctor will look at the full blood count and may suggest further tests.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- What does my red cell count tell you when you look at it with my haemoglobin?
- Are my red cells a normal size, judging by my MCV?
- Should I have this test repeated?
