## What it measures
HbA1c shows your **average blood sugar level over the past two to three months**. When sugar is in the blood, some of it sticks to the haemoglobin inside red blood cells. Red cells live for about three months, so the share of haemoglobin with sugar attached reflects how high blood sugar has been, on average, over that time. The result is given as a percentage, and sometimes also in mmol/mol.

## Why the test is done
HbA1c is used to screen for and diagnose prediabetes and diabetes, and to check how well blood sugar is being managed in people who have diabetes. Unlike a fasting glucose test, it does not need fasting, and it is not much affected by a single meal or a single day.

## Understanding the reference range
Commonly used guides are: normal, below 5.7%; prediabetes, 5.7% to 6.4%; and diabetes range, 6.5% or above (usually confirmed with a repeat test). In mmol/mol these are roughly below 39, 39 to 47, and 48 or above. People with diabetes are often given a personal target by their doctor. Some conditions that affect red blood cells, such as anaemia, recent blood loss, pregnancy or certain inherited haemoglobin types, can make HbA1c less reliable.

## What high values can be associated with
An HbA1c above the normal range means blood sugar has been higher than usual over recent months. It can be associated with prediabetes or diabetes. In some cases, iron deficiency or kidney conditions can raise the result. Only your doctor can tell what your result means for you.

## What low values can be associated with
A low HbA1c is uncommon. It can be associated with conditions in which red blood cells are replaced faster than usual, such as recent blood loss or some types of anaemia, and with frequent low blood sugar in people treated for diabetes.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- Is my HbA1c in the normal, prediabetes or diabetes range?
- Could anything, such as anaemia, affect how accurate my HbA1c is?
- How often should I have this test repeated?
- What target should I aim for?
