## What it measures
Electrolytes are minerals in the blood that carry a small electric charge. They help control the amount of water in the body, keep nerves and muscles (including the heart) working, and keep the blood's acid balance steady. An electrolyte panel usually measures:
- **Sodium (Na):** mainly controls the balance of water in and around cells.
- **Potassium (K):** is vital for nerve signals and for a steady heartbeat.
- **Chloride (Cl)** and **bicarbonate (HCO₃)**, which help with fluid and acid balance.
Results are usually given in mmol/L or mEq/L, which are equal for these minerals.

## Why the test is done
Electrolytes are part of routine metabolic panels. Doctors check them when someone has vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration, weakness, confusion or an irregular heartbeat, and to monitor kidney conditions, heart conditions, and medicines that affect fluid balance, such as diuretics (water tablets).

## Understanding the reference range
Typical adult ranges are about 136 to 145 mmol/L for sodium and 3.5 to 5.1 mmol/L for potassium, but labs differ. The body keeps these values within narrow limits, so even small changes can matter more than for some other tests. Potassium can look falsely high if the blood sample was handled roughly or left too long before testing. Doctors may repeat the test to check. A lab may mark very high or very low values as critical.

## What high values can be associated with
High sodium can be associated with dehydration or not drinking enough water. High potassium can be associated with reduced kidney function, some blood pressure and heart medicines, damage to the blood sample, and some hormone conditions. Very high potassium can affect the heart rhythm, which is why labs flag it as critical. Your doctor can tell what applies to you.

## What low values can be associated with
Low sodium can be associated with drinking a very large amount of water, vomiting or diarrhoea, some medicines, and heart, liver or kidney conditions. Low potassium can be associated with vomiting, diarrhoea, diuretics and some hormone conditions. Only your doctor can interpret these results for you.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- Is my sodium or potassium far enough outside the range to need prompt action?
- Could any of my medicines be affecting my electrolytes?
- Should the test be repeated in case the sample affected the result?
