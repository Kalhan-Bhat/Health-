## What it measures
When your body breaks down protein from food, it makes a waste product called urea in the liver. Urea travels in the blood to the kidneys, which filter it out into urine. The **blood urea nitrogen (BUN)** test measures the amount of nitrogen from urea in your blood. Some labs report **urea** instead of BUN. These are two ways of describing the same substance, and urea values are roughly twice BUN values when both are in mg/dL.

## Why the test is done
BUN or urea is usually checked together with creatinine as part of kidney function tests or a metabolic panel. It helps doctors understand how well the kidneys are working and whether a person may be dehydrated. Some reports also show a **BUN to creatinine ratio**, which can help point towards possible reasons for an abnormal result.

## Understanding the reference range
A common adult BUN range is about 7 to 20 mg/dL, and a common urea range is about 15 to 40 mg/dL, but labs differ. BUN is affected by many things besides the kidneys, such as how much protein you eat, how much fluid you drink, and your age. For this reason, doctors rarely look at BUN on its own.

## What high values can be associated with
A BUN above the range can be associated with dehydration, a high-protein diet, reduced kidney function, heart failure, bleeding in the stomach or intestines, some medicines such as steroids, and illness or stress. Only your doctor can tell what applies to you.

## What low values can be associated with
A BUN below the range is less common and usually not a concern. It can be associated with a low-protein diet, drinking a lot of fluid, pregnancy, and, less often, liver conditions.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- What does my BUN or urea result mean when you look at it with my creatinine?
- Could my diet or how much water I drink have affected it?
- Do I need any further kidney tests?
