## What it measures
Glucose is a type of sugar and the body's main source of energy. It comes from the food you eat, especially carbohydrates. A hormone called insulin, made by the pancreas, helps move glucose from the blood into cells. The fasting blood glucose test measures the amount of glucose in your blood after you have not eaten for at least 8 hours, usually overnight. Some reports also show **PPBS** (blood sugar about two hours after a meal) or a **random blood sugar** taken at any time.

## Why the test is done
Doctors use fasting glucose to screen for prediabetes and diabetes, to check people with symptoms such as strong thirst, passing a lot of urine or unexplained weight loss, and to monitor people who already have diabetes. It is often part of routine health checks, especially for adults with risk factors such as excess weight or a family history of diabetes.

## Understanding the reference range
Commonly used guides for fasting glucose are: normal, 70 to 99 mg/dL; prediabetes, 100 to 125 mg/dL; and diabetes range, 126 mg/dL or more on two separate tests. In mmol/L, 100 mg/dL is about 5.6 and 126 mg/dL is about 7.0. A single result is not enough for a diagnosis. Doctors usually confirm it with a repeat test or with HbA1c. Eating before a fasting test will make the result look higher.

## What high values can be associated with
A fasting glucose above the range can be associated with prediabetes or diabetes. It can also be associated with not fasting properly, physical stress from illness or surgery, some medicines such as steroids, and some hormone conditions. Only your doctor can tell what your result means for you.

## What low values can be associated with
A fasting glucose below the range (hypoglycaemia) is less common in people without diabetes. It can be associated with a long fast, heavy exercise, alcohol, some diabetes medicines, and, rarely, hormone or liver conditions. Signs of low sugar can include shakiness, sweating, confusion and a fast heartbeat.

## Questions you might ask your doctor
- Is my fasting glucose in the normal, prediabetes or diabetes range?
- Should I have an HbA1c test or repeat this test to confirm it?
- How often should my blood sugar be checked?
